## 1. Curriculum promise

This is not an easier substitute for an academic linguistics roadmap. It is an engineered entrance to one.

A learner with no background should be able to attempt the first question immediately. By the end of a route, that learner should be able to recognize technical vocabulary, follow a formal explanation, read an authentic introductory source with purpose, and transfer the idea to unfamiliar language data.

The public roadmap is therefore organized by questions a newcomer can understand. An academic concept graph sits underneath it.

```text
familiar question
→ prediction
→ contrast or surprise
→ pattern stated in ordinary language
→ academic term
→ formal model
→ guided source
→ unfamiliar problem
```

The academic vocabulary is delayed only until it has a visible referent. It is never removed.

## 2. First encounter: translation is not replacement

The roadmap opens with a prediction rather than a definition.
```text
One English word. Two ordinary Chinese translations.

brother → 哥哥 / 弟弟

Which is correct?
[哥哥] [弟弟] [Not enough context]
```

After the learner chooses, show two contexts:

```text
My brother is three years older than I am. → 哥哥
My brother just started primary school.     → 弟弟
```

The first plain-language discovery is:

> English leaves the age relation unstated in *brother*. Chinese commonly makes that relation explicit in the kin term. Translation cannot be simple label replacement; the translator must reconstruct an interpretation and express the distinctions required by another language.

The academic vocabulary then has work to do:

- **lexicalization:** the conventional encoding of a concept in a lexical item;
- **semantic field:** a structured domain of related meanings;
- **contextual enrichment:** contextually licensed information beyond conventionally encoded sentence meaning;
- **translation equivalence:** correspondence across semantic, pragmatic, stylistic, or functional dimensions rather than total identity.

The learner has not been given a watered-down definition. They have produced the problem the definition solves.

## 3. How a beginner reaches later texts

Every concept page uses the same six-part ramp.

| Stage | Learner action | Academic growth | Interface requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Notice | Predict, sort, imitate, or choose | An observable phenomenon becomes a question | 30–90 second interaction with no required terminology |
| 2. Compare | Isolate a changing feature | Evidence replaces impression | Highlighted contrast or aligned examples |
| 3. Name | Attach a stable label to the discovered pattern | Technical vocabulary enters | Term, pronunciation if needed, and one-sentence definition |
| 4. Formalize | Use a table, tree, rule, feature, or notation | Intuition becomes a reproducible analysis | Worked representation plus a common wrong turn |
| 5. Read | Enter an authentic text with guiding questions | The learner encounters disciplinary prose | Glossary, reading purpose, and readiness cues |
| 6. Solve | Apply the idea to different surface data | Understanding transfers | Start Here, Practice, and Challenge problems |

### Reading should be continuous, not a jump

The learning interface should not end a friendly explanation and then send the learner to a wall of academic prose. It should prepare a specific bridge:

1. “You have already observed **X**.”
2. “The reading calls this **Y**.”
3. “In the first section, find the author's formal definition of **Y**.”
4. “Compare it with the rule you proposed.”
5. “Record one case the definition explains and one limit or unresolved case.”

Every recommended source must say why the learner is ready for it, not merely display a link.

## 4. Section 01 · Introduction in full

Section title: **Introduction**

Visible route question: **Why can one word need two translations?**
Academic subtitle: **Lexical semantics · Pragmatics · Translation**

| Step | Beginner-facing question | Academic destination | Evidence or task | Reading bridge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Which brother is it? | Lexicalization | Choose 哥哥, 弟弟, or insufficient context | *Lexicalization* now names the difference the learner found |
| 2 | What did the sentence leave unsaid? | Contextual enrichment | Compare older-than and primary-school contexts | Separate encoded meaning from contextual interpretation |
| 3 | Do words draw the same borders? | Semantic fields | Diagram sibling, brother, 哥哥, and 弟弟 as overlapping regions | Read field diagrams as partitions rather than translation pairs |
| 4 | Where does an interpretation come from? | Form, meaning, reference, context | Analyze the two readings of *bank* | Use the distinctions assumed in semantics and pragmatics texts |
| 5 | What should a translation preserve? | Translation equivalence | Translate 您 and 你 while recording lost social information | Evaluate semantic, pragmatic, and stylistic correspondence separately |
| 6 | Is this pattern bigger than two languages? | Semantic typology | Compare one kinship contrast across three languages | Read typological claims with attention to coding and evidence |

This route can lead directly into three neighboring sections:

```text
01 · Introduction: translation is not replacement
├── 02 · Decode from Evidence
├── 07 · Meaning in Context
└── 08 · Lexical Worlds
```

## 5. Learner-facing atlas

The atlas is comprehensive through coverage, not through an intimidating opening taxonomy. It first presents these sections as one macro-scale network. Each node gives a stable section title; each searchable card adds the beginner-facing question and academic subtitle.

| No. | Section title | Doorway question | Immediate hook | Academic core | Destination |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|

| 02 | Decode from Evidence | How can I decode a language I have never learned? | Six unknown forms and translations | Alignment, controlled contrast, segmentation, hypothesis testing, counterevidence, glossing | Data-based linguistic analysis |
| 03 | Word Structure | How can one word contain a whole sentence? | One word translates as a whole event | Morphemes, roots, affixes, morphotactics, inflection, derivation, agreement, case, polysynthesis | Morphology and interlinear texts |
| 04 | Sentence Structure | How do languages show who did what? | Actor recoverable without familiar word order | Semantic roles, constituent order, constituency, case, agreement, null arguments, alignment | Syntax and morphosyntax |
| 05 | Sound Systems | Why do languages hear and organize sounds differently? | A contrast for one listener is “the same” for another | Articulatory phonetics, phonemes, allophones, processes, prosody, IPA | Introductory phonetics and phonology |
| 06 | Writing Systems | How can marks carry language? | A bird sign may have several jobs | Glottography, sign values, inventories, mixed scripts, decipherment, parallel texts | Writing systems and historical decipherment |

At the macro scale, Section 01 branches rather than becoming a single compulsory sequence. The structural, interpretive, social, cognitive, computational, and fieldwork branches repeatedly reconnect; Section 14 makes that synthesis explicit. These are explanatory relationships, not enrollment gates.

Question routes are views over a shared concept network. They must not become isolated mini-courses.

| Academic area | Primary route | Important cross-links |

| Morphology and lexicon | Words | Sentences, sound, typology, olympiad |
| Syntax and morphosyntax | Sentences | Meaning, typology, computation, olympiad |
| Semantics | Translation; interpretation | Lexical worlds, mind, computation |
| Pragmatics and discourse | Interpretation | Translation, society, communication design |
| Writing systems | Marks | History, decoding, olympiad |
| Typology | Diversity and change | Every structural route |
| Historical linguistics and contact | Diversity and change | Sound, words, society, fieldwork |
| Sociolinguistics | Society | Interpretation, history, documentation |
| Acquisition and psycholinguistics | Mind | Sound, grammar, meaning, computation |
| Computational linguistics | Computation | Every representational layer |
| Field methods and documentation | Speakers and communities | Variation, ethics, every structural layer |
| Analytical method | Decoding; olympiad | Shared across all routes |

When a concept appears in multiple routes, it has one canonical record, one glossary definition, one progress state, and route-specific framing.

Example:

```text
canonical concept: segmentation

shown in “decode an unknown language” as:
Where should I split the expression?

shown in “one word contains a sentence” as:
Which pieces keep returning?

shown in “computers work with language” as:
What should count as one unit?
```

The frames differ; the learner can see the deeper relation.

## 7. Depth standard for every concept

Every implemented concept must contain three continuous layers.

### A. Orientation

- a hook requiring no field knowledge;
- one prediction or manipulation;
- a 60–120 word reveal;
- one explicit learning outcome.

### B. Core lesson

- 500–1,200 words;
- terminology introduced in use;
- a worked dataset or representation;
- a common but reasonable wrong analysis;
- a transfer example from different surface data;
- a self-check that asks for reasoning.

### C. Academic depth

- precise definitions and notation;
- the assumptions of the analysis;
- competing analyses where genuinely relevant;
- interfaces with neighboring fields;
- source notes and further reading;
- difficult LingoHub problems.

Plain-language framing may reduce unnecessary opacity. It must not erase uncertainty, replace evidence with analogy, or pretend disputed analyses are settled.

## 8. Problem attachment

Problems are attached to concepts through an explicit relation rather than inferred from broad tags.

Each attachment records:

- the concept practised;
- whether the concept is **introduced**, **required**, or **extended**;
- why the mapping is valid;
- the recommended stage: Start Here, Practice, or Challenge;
- any additional prerequisites that make the problem substantially easier to enter.

The first problem after a lesson should vary surface details while preserving the central relationship. A second problem should combine the concept with another route. A Challenge problem may remove scaffolds entirely.

## 9. Beginner test

Before publishing a route, test it with people who have not taken linguistics.

A route succeeds only if a new learner can:

1. understand the opening question without definitions;
2. make a meaningful attempt;
3. state the discovered contrast in ordinary language;
4. explain what the academic term names;
5. use the term correctly on a second example;
6. identify the relevant passage in the guided reading;
7. begin the first attached problem without needing hidden prerequisites.

If learners can repeat a definition but cannot analyze the transfer example, the ramp has failed. If they enjoy the hook but never meet the formal account, it has also failed.
